Subject: Every machine he owns just started pulsing to the same rhythm

The end of the world sounds like a whisper.

Imagine you've spent years hiding at the end of four miles of cracked road on the fog-bound Oregon coast. You have a dead NOAA weather station, an eight-year-old Malinois with a bad hip he'll never admit to, and a duct-taped android who steeps tea he can't drink.

Then every machine you own starts pulsing to the same rhythm.

That's how The Quiet Frequency opens for Slade Quintz, a burned-out spy with a permanent whine in his ears and nine years of guilt he's been carefully not looking at.

The rhythm turns out to be a voice. A dying colony of living light, high above Earth, blacking out the grid every time it cries for help. Sebastion, the android, has already named it Lumen, because calling it "it" seemed rude.

To answer it, Slade needs the one person he left behind: Dr. Marisol Vega, the physicist whose paper on living plasma in the sky was dismissed as "a fairy tale in LaTeX." A storm is rolling in. A federal director with clean shoes and a looping signature has other plans for Lumen, and they involve a weapon.

A dying alien mind. A burned-out spy. One last signal.

**What it's like to read:**
- First-person, present tense, with dry humor sitting right on top of real heartbreak
- Sebastion's deadpan butler wit against Slade's guilt
- Quiet and atmospheric at the start, then a full thriller once the feds arrive
- One week of fog and storm, a sealed Cold War listening bunker, and an observatory in Eugene

**Who it's for:** If you like wry, character-driven first-contact sci-fi, or burned-out-spy stories where the hardest thing isn't the mission, this is written with you in mind. For Slade, the hardest part is staying.
