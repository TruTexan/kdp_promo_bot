# The Man Who Still Writes the Tide Log

## A Station Nobody Needs

Cape Sorrow is a dead NOAA weather station at the end of four miles of cracked road above Port Halloran, on the fog-bound Oregon coast. The instruments are decommissioned. The forecasts stopped years ago. No agency is waiting on its numbers.

Slade Quintz writes the tide log anyway. By hand, every morning.

## Why a Retired Spy Keeps Records

Slade is a retired spy with a permanent whine in his ears from a Tbilisi stairwell and nine years of guilt over Lisbon. He came to Cape Sorrow to disappear, bringing his Malinois, Cipher, and a duct-taped android named Sebastion, who steeps tea he can't drink.

The log is small, and that's the point. Tides arrive on schedule. They don't ask who you used to be. For a man whose old job was reading people for lies, a column of honest numbers is a kind of rest.

## Then the Machines Start Pulsing

In *The Quiet Frequency*, that routine breaks when every machine Slade owns begins pulsing to the same rhythm. It turns out to be a voice: a dying colony of living light high above Earth, blacking out the grid each time it cries for help. Sebastion names it Lumen, because calling it "it" seems rude.

Answering Lumen means turning to Dr. Marisol Vega, the woman Slade left behind nine years ago. Meanwhile a storm is building, and Director Amelia Hartwell sees the colony as a weapon.

## A Quiet Kind of Thriller

The book is told in first person, present tense, and pairs dry humor with real heartbreak. Sebastion's deadpan butler wit balances Slade's guilt. The mood stays hushed and foggy until the feds arrive and the pace tightens.

If you like character-driven, wry first-contact sci-fi or burned-out-spy thrillers, this one may suit you.

*The end of the world sounds like a whisper.*
