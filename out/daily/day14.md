THE QUIET FREQUENCY by Jules Rooke
A dying alien mind. A burned-out spy. One last signal.

Here's what I think makes it worth your evening:

• First contact that's really about listening. Can you hear someone who's asking for help? Can you be heard when you've spent years making yourself impossible to find?

• Dry humor over real heartbreak. Slade narrates in first person, present tense, with a whine in his ears from a Tbilisi stairwell and nine years of guilt over Lisbon. Sebastion's deadpan butler wit keeps the weight bearable.

• Found family with a bad hip and a tea set. A man, a dog, and a robot who can't drink what he brews: the odd little household at the heart of the book.

• A second chance that won't be easy. To answer Lumen, Slade needs Dr. Marisol Vega, the physicist whose paper on living plasma in the sky was dismissed as "a fairy tale in LaTeX", and the woman he left behind nine years ago.

• A slow-burn atmosphere that turns into a thriller. The first stretch is fog, static, and quiet. Then a storm rolls in, the feds arrive, and Director Amelia Hartwell, with her clean shoes and looping signature, has a plan to turn Lumen into a weapon.

The whole story unfolds over one week of fog and storm, from a lonely coastal station to a sealed Cold War listening bunker to an observatory in Eugene. Saving Lumen will cost everything. For Slade, the hardest part is staying.
