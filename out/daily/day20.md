Why did I write The Quiet Frequency?

Because I kept wondering what first contact would look like if it didn't arrive with warships. What if it came as a whisper? What if the voice was dying, and the only people who noticed were the ones who'd already given up on the world?

That's how I found Slade Quintz, a retired spy hiding at a decommissioned weather station on the foggy Oregon coast. He has a Malinois with a bad hip he'll never admit to, and an android named Sebastion who is held together with duct tape and steeps tea he can't drink. Then every machine Slade owns starts pulsing to the same rhythm, and it turns out to be a cry for help from a colony of living light high above Earth.

To answer it, he needs the woman he abandoned nine years ago. A storm is coming, powerful people want the signal as a weapon, and for Slade the hardest part is staying.

It's a quiet, atmospheric, funny-and-heartbreaking science fiction thriller, written in first person, present tense.

Title: I wrote a first-contact story where the alien isn't invading, it's dying. Here's why I wanted to write it that way.

Body:
I've been thinking about why so many first-contact stories are about the *arrival*: the ships, the countdown, the generals in rooms. I wanted to write about the part nobody films, which is what happens when the thing that finds us is quiet, scared, and running out of time.

That's where my novel, The Quiet Frequency, started. The question was: what if the signal isn't a threat or a greeting, but a cry for help, and the only person in range to hear it is someone who has spent years making sure nobody can find him?

A few things I was trying to do:

**1. Make the cosmic story small and personal.**
My protagonist, Slade Quintz, is a retired spy hiding in a dead NOAA weather station on the Oregon coast with a bad-hipped ex-federal Malinois named Cipher and an android concierge named Sebastion, whom he pulled out of a scrapyard for $20 and a six-pack. When every machine he owns starts pulsing to the same rhythm, it turns out to be a voice: a dying colony of living light high above Earth, blacking out the grid each time it calls out. I wanted the biggest event in human history to land in the most cramped, lonely, duct-taped corner of the world.

**2. Let humor and grief share a room.**
The book is first-person, present tense. Slade's guilt is real, and so is the ringing in his ears from a Tbilisi stairwell. Sebastion is there to be a deadpan butler about it all, steeping tea he can't drink and fixing everything with duct tape. Dry wit is how a lot of people I know get through heavy things. I wanted that on the page without it ever cancelling out the heartbreak.

**3. Make staying the hard part.**
Slade is great at leaving. To answer the signal he needs the physicist he walked away from nine years ago, the one whose paper on living plasma in the sky got dismissed as "a fairy tale in LaTeX." Saving something that's dying isn't the hardest thing for him. Staying put long enough to try is.

**4. Start quiet, then tighten the screws.**
The first stretch is fog, static, and atmosphere. Then the feds arrive, a storm rolls in, and it becomes a thriller, because some people with clean shoes would very much like to turn a living thing into a weapon.

I'm curious what the sub thinks:

It's a character-driven sci-fi thriller in the vein of wry first contact meets burned-out-spy story. If the mods are fine with it, I'm happy to share where to find it. Otherwise I'm glad to just talk craft, tropes, or recs in the comments.
