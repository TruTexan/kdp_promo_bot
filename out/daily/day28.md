Hi there,

A small thing has been happening since The Quiet Frequency went out to early readers, and it's making me grin.

They keep finishing it in one sitting.

Nobody planned that. The book opens quietly: fog, a dead weather station on the Oregon coast, and a retired spy who has spent years making sure nothing finds him. Then every machine he owns starts pulsing to the same rhythm, and the quiet stops being safe.

WHAT'S INSIDE

Slade Quintz is 56, with a permanent whine in his ears from a Tbilisi stairwell and nine years of guilt over Lisbon. He's hiding at Cape Sorrow with:

- Cipher, an eight-year-old Malinois with a federal tattoo and a bad hip he'll never admit hurts
- Sebastion, a duct-taped concierge robot rescued from a Coos Bay scrapyard for $20 and a six-pack, who steeps tea he can't drink

The pulse is a voice. It belongs to a dying colony of living light high above Earth, one that blacks out the grid every time it cries for help. Sebastion named it Lumen, because calling it "it" seemed rude.

To answer it, Slade needs Dr. Marisol Vega, the physicist whose paper was dismissed as "a fairy tale in LaTeX," and the woman he abandoned nine years ago. A storm is coming. Director Amelia Hartwell, with her clean shoes and her looping signature, has other plans for Lumen.

WHY IT MAY GO DOWN IN ONE GO

- It's first-person, present tense, so you're in Slade's head with no time to catch your breath.
- The humor is dry and the heartbreak is real. Sebastion's deadpan butler wit keeps Slade's guilt from swallowing the page.
- The whole story takes place over one week of fog and storm. It starts quiet and atmospheric, then turns into a full thriller once the feds arrive.

If you like character-driven, wry first-contact sci-fi, or burned-out-spy stories with an actual heart, this one is for you.

The end of the world sounds like a whisper.
