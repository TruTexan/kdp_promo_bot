Title: A robot who steeps tea he can't drink: what makes a machine character feel like a person instead of a gimmick?

Body:

I've been thinking about this while working on a first-contact thriller, and I'd like to hear how other people handle it.

One of my main characters is Sebastion, the last surviving hotel-concierge robot. He was found face down in a Coos Bay scrapyard and bought for $20 and a six-pack. He now lives in a dead weather station on the Oregon coast and repairs everything with duct tape. He also steeps tea every day, even though he can't drink it.

The tea ended up carrying more characterization than any backstory I wrote for him. Here's what I learned:

1. **Give the machine a ritual that doesn't serve a function.** The tea does nothing for him. He does it because it's what a good host does, and that tells you who he is without a speech about loyalty.

2. **Let the programming be the personality, then let it leak.** A concierge is built to anticipate needs and stay calm. When the person he looks after is falling apart, that training starts to look a lot like love.

3. **Deadpan is a good pressure valve.** My narrator is a burned-out ex-spy carrying a lot of guilt, so the book needed someone who could be dry and funny without undercutting the real emotion. Sebastion's butler-style understatement lets the sad parts stay sad.

4. **Imperfection helps.** Duct tape is a visible, ongoing choice to keep something running instead of replacing it. It says a lot about the household without anyone explaining it.

5. **Small kindnesses beat big gestures.** At one point he names a threat in the sky because calling it "it" seemed rude. That one line does more for his character than a dramatic rescue would.

Some of my favorite machine characters work this way: Murderbot, Data, Wall-E, the Iron Giant. None of them are interesting because they're almost human. They're interesting because they're specific, and their quirks make them feel like they have a past.

A few questions for the thread:

- Which robot or AI character felt most alive to you, and what was the detail that sold it?
- Do you prefer machines who want to be human, or machines who are comfortable being machines?
- Is there a quirk that made you roll your eyes because it felt like a gimmick?
