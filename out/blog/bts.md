# The Whine Is the Point

## A Note From Cape Sorrow

I hear a pitch no one else in the room can hear. It sits just behind my left ear, thin and steady, like a wet finger circling a glass that never sings out. It has been there for years. I stopped asking it to leave around the time I stopped asking most things for most things.

"You're doing the face again," says Sebastion, from the doorway. He is an android held together with duct tape and what I can only call spite. "The one where you listen to nothing very hard."

"It's not nothing."

"Medically, sir, that is exactly what it is."

He's right, and that's the problem with him.

## Why It Matters

When I tell people about the book, I mean the story where I'm the main character, which is an awkward thing to admit, they assume the tinnitus is set dressing. A haunted spy needs a haunting. A ringing ear is cheap atmosphere, like fog on a cliff.

It isn't. In this story, the thing that has tormented me for years is the reason I notice anything is wrong at all.

Cape Sorrow is a dead weather station on the Oregon coast, and I chose it because nothing happens here. Wind, salt, and a dog named Cipher who believes every gull is a personal insult. Then the machines start. The radio. The old console. Things I haven't plugged in. All of them begin to pulse to one rhythm, and it takes me embarrassingly long to realize I've been hearing that rhythm for years, buried in the whine.

Nobody else could have caught it. They'd have called it interference and moved on.

## The Quiet Part

I like the first stretch of this story best, though "like" is generous. It's quiet. Surf, static, a man pretending his life is a choice. Sebastion narrates my failures in the tone of a concierge reading a bill. Cipher leans on my shin like a sandbag.

Underneath, something is crying out. It isn't a ghost or a glitch. It's a voice from a dying colony of living light, high above Earth, and every time it calls for help, the grid goes dark.

Answering it means finding someone I walked away from nine years ago. I'd rather face a firing squad. A firing squad has better manners.

## Then the Feds Arrive

I won't say much about this part, except that the quiet doesn't last. A storm is coming, and powerful people have decided the colony would make a fine weapon. Black SUVs and polite threats show up on a road that barely deserves the name, and the book shifts gears hard.

"Shall I hide the good silverware, sir?" Sebastion asks.

"We don't have good silverware."

"Then they'll be disappointed in more ways than one."

I've spent years being very good at leaving. What I'm learning, painfully, is that the signal doesn't need a man who can run. It needs one who can stay.
