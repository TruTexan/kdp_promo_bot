# Notes from Cape Sorrow

## The Whine

There's a sound in my head that isn't there. It started in a stairwell in Tbilisi, and it has kept me company ever since. Doctors call it tinnitus. I call it my roommate. It never pays rent and it never leaves.

I'm 56 and retired, which in my old profession means I haven't been shot at lately. I live at Cape Sorrow, a dead weather station on the Oregon coast, where the wind does most of the talking. I came here because nobody visits a place named Sorrow on purpose.

## The Household

My household is small. Cipher is a dog who judges me with her whole face. Sebastion is an android held together by duct tape and stubbornness.

"Sir, you've been staring at the horizon for forty minutes," he says this morning. "I'd call it brooding, but the horizon hasn't done anything to deserve it."

"It knows what it did."

"I'm sure it does. Your tea is cold. I've made another. You won't drink that one either, but it's the principle."

He's the closest thing I have to a conscience that comes with a warranty. Mine ran out around the time of Lisbon. Nine years, and I still don't say the word out loud.

## The Rhythm

Then the machines start to pulse.

First it's the radio, a soft throb under the static. Then the old monitors, then the kettle, then Sebastion himself, whose eyes flicker in time with something I can't hear. Everything I own beats to the same quiet rhythm, as if the whole station has grown a heart.

It isn't a glitch. It's a voice.

Somewhere far above the Earth, a colony of living light is dying, and every time it cries for help, the grid goes dark. I'd like to say I ignore it. I try. Cipher, who has better instincts than I do, sits by the door and waits.

"It seems to be asking for something, sir," Sebastion says.

"Everything's asking for something."

"Yes, but this one is asking politely, and you've always been weak for manners."

## The Part I Don't Want to Write

Answering it means finding the woman I abandoned nine years ago. I'd rather face the stairwell again. At least the stairwell never looked at me like she will.

Meanwhile the quiet at Cape Sorrow starts to crack. A storm is building offshore. Then come the cars on the gravel road, the polished shoes, and the feds, who announce themselves with the kind of smile that has a file behind it. They've noticed the pulse too. They don't want to save the colony. They want to own it, and they are not the only powerful people who do.

So the slow, foggy book I thought I was living in turns into a race. I'm rusty, I'm guilty, and my ears are ringing. My butler is held together with tape.

## What I've Learned So Far

Running was always the one thing I was good at. Saving something, it turns out, may require the opposite. It may require staying, and that is the hardest assignment I've ever been handed.
